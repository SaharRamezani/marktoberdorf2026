The Kernel, Proof Automation and AI

Lecture by Leonardo de Moura, the designer of Lean, from his deck Proof Automation and AI, the third of his four. It follows the deck, authoritative for the technical content, together with the recording, which supplies what he said around the slides. The audio is damaged in places, so nothing is quoted from it directly.

The deck opens on the obvious objection: if a model can write proofs, why keep building tactics? Three tactics follow, simp, grind and bv_decide, then what AI is doing with Lean today, and finally the kernel and what the green check mark in the editor means .

Why proof automation did not become obsolete

The answer is short and it sets up everything else . Tactics are the moves of the game, for a model as much as for a person. Better tactics give shorter proofs, shorter proofs mean smaller search trees, so a model gets further on the same budget, and compact proofs make better training data. Automation did not become obsolete, it became infrastructure .

Rewriting against a database, and where it stops

simp rewrites the goal with every lemma carrying the @[simp] attribute, left to right, until no rule applies . Two design rules make a good set: each lemma's left side should be more complex than its right, so rewriting makes progress towards a normal form, and the set must be confluent enough that the firing order does not change the answer . The limit arrives quickly. No rewrite rule solves a linear system, and equalities have to travel between hypotheses, through function applications and into case splits rather than only downwards through one goal . That combination, rewriting plus theories plus propagation, is what grind is.

The whiteboard

grind shipped in Lean v4.22 and is the work of Kim Morrison and de Moura. The image used for it is a virtual whiteboard, borrowed from modern SMT solvers: facts are written on the board, equivalent terms are merged, and several engines read from it and write back . The board is an E-graph, with equivalence classes kept by union-find, so a = b merges the class of a with the class of b and congruence does the rest: equal arguments give equal applications, so f a and f b merge too. Nothing is rewritten, terms are only related, which is the difference between this and simp .

Four engines cooperate there, congruence closure, E-matching, constraint propagation and guided case analysis, with satellite theory solvers alongside. grind is native to dependent type theory, so nothing is translated into first-order logic and reconstructed on the way back, and it produces ordinary Lean proof terms, so the kernel checks its output like anyone else's .

How the annotations steer it

E-matching is where library knowledge enters, and the attributes are how an author supplies it. A theorem annotated @[grind =] becomes an instantiation rule whose pattern is its left-hand side, matched modulo the equalities already on the board rather than against the syntax of the goal . The small example shows why that word carries the idea. With f (g x) = x annotated, and hypotheses f a = b and a = g c, the term f (g c) appears nowhere. The board knows a = g c, so f a matches f (g ?x), the instance f (g c) = c lands on the board, and congruence chains b = f a = f (g c) = c. A lemma fires when the equalities match, not when the syntax does.

The annotations come in three directions. [grind =] takes the pattern from the left side of an equation. [grind →] marks a forward rule: the patterns come from the premises, so when matching facts appear on the board the conclusion is added to it. [grind ←] is the dual, taking its patterns from the conclusion. Transitivity is the canonical forward case, stated for divisibility .

def divides (a b : Nat) : Prop := ∃ k, b = a * k

@[grind →] theorem divides_trans (h₁ : divides a b) (h₂ : divides b c) :
    divides a c := by
  obtain ⟨k₁, rfl⟩ := h₁
  obtain ⟨k₂, rfl⟩ := h₂
  exact ⟨k₁ * k₂, Nat.mul_assoc a k₁ k₂⟩

example (h₁ : divides a b) (h₂ : divides b c) (h₃ : divides c d) :
    divides a d := by
  grind

Two firings of that rule chain the three hypotheses into divides a d. Forward chains can grow without bound, so grind bounds its instantiation rounds and its diagnostics list every instance created. Making the tactic an expert in your own library costs two lines: a small state API is annotated with a lemma for reading back what was just written and a lemma for reading past an unrelated write, after which nested updates are discharged without help .

Theory solvers, and how they meet

The satellite solvers engage automatically when the right type classes are present, which is the dependent-type-theory way of dispatching on a domain : cutsat for linear integer arithmetic over Int, Nat, Int32, BitVec n and Fin n, a Gröbner basis solver wherever a commutative ring or field is declared, linarith over ordered structures, and AC reasoning for any associative and commutative operator. cutsat is complete for its fragment and the workhorse for indices, sizes and bounds , while the ring solver reaches further than its name suggests: BitVec 8 is a commutative ring of characteristic 256, so (x - 16) * (x + 16) = x ^ 2 is proved by normalisation with no bitvector theory . One slide shows three of them meeting on a single goal : the ring solver derives a^4 + b^4 = 9 - c^4, congruence closure lifts that equality through an arbitrary function, and linear arithmetic closes what is left. That is the Nelson-Oppen playbook, inside dependent type theory rather than across a translation into SMT.

What it reaches, and what it does when it fails

Case analysis is handled apart, with splits on match and if expressions and on annotated disjunctions under heuristics against explosion: case analysis is where automation costs blow up, and controlling it is half the work . For reach the deck gives one number, more than five thousand grind calls in Mathlib , and is explicit about what the tactic is not : not designed for combinatorially explosive search, not a translation to an external SMT solver, which is claimed as an advantage because there is no encoding gap out and no reconstruction gap back, and not a nonlinear arithmetic oracle, since the ring solver normalises polynomial equalities while hard nonlinear inequalities stay hard.

The failure behaviour is a feature. When grind fails it prints its board, the facts it knew, the classes it built and the instances it tried, so the missing piece can be identified rather than guessed at. In the failing example nothing rules out an array index j equalling the updated index i, where the claim is false. The loop is fail, read, fix, succeed .

bv_decide, and the number worth keeping

Fixed-width machine arithmetic is where hand proofs are least pleasant and bugs most common, and the ring solver handles only part of it: complete answers need a decision procedure . bv_decide bit-blasts the goal to SAT, runs CaDiCaL, and then declines to believe the answer: it replays the solver's LRAT certificate through a checker verified in Lean, so the SAT solver is never trusted and the kernel still checks everything. Being a decision procedure it answers both ways, returning a counterexample when the claim is false .

The scale claim is the figure worth keeping. Benchmarked against Bitwuzla on 46,191 SMT-LIB bit-vector problems, bv_decide solves 45,046, 97.5 per cent, with kernel checking included, against Bitwuzla's 45,817; the two agree on every problem both solved, and total CPU time is within 2.3 times Bitwuzla's . A verified pipeline competitive with an unverified state of the art solver would not have been a credible claim a few years ago.

What AI is already doing with Lean

Every medal-level olympiad system that produces formal proofs uses Lean, and one of AlphaProof's own moves is shown to be an ordinary tactic script . The same pattern runs through an Erdős prize problem resolved with a machine-checked proof written with a chatbot's help , a leaderboard of formalisation submissions on hard problems , and Tau Ceti, AI-authored Lean mathematics on a human-owned roadmap gated by open adversarial review : humans own the specifications, agents iterate on the code and the proofs .

"It's really important with these formal proof assistants that there are no backdoors or exploits you can use to somehow get your certified proof without actually proving it, because reinforcement learning is just so good at finding these backdoors."

Terence Tao, quoted on

A reinforcement learning system optimises the checker, not the mathematics, so a tool with a large trusted base becomes a liability, and the kernel architecture matters more now than before anything was searching it .

What the check mark means

The reference manual gained a chapter on levels of trust about a year before the lecture, because people were confusing them: users would say a result had been checked without being able to say which of several quite different things they meant. Against an honest mistake the check mark is a real result; against an author trying to deceive you it is close to worthless, and the manual now says so.

The check mark in the IDE: the proof is complete, with no placeholder in the theorem you are looking at. It assumes an honest author, with nothing sent to the kernel behind its back.

#print axioms, transitive over the dependency graph, so it catches a placeholder in something you cite and a non-standard axiom added below you.

lean4checker re-runs the official kernel over the compiled output rather than the editor session, out of reach of anything that happened while you were editing.

The gold standard. lean4export writes the development out and independent kernels re-check it: nanoda in Rust and lean4lean in Lean, beside the official C++ one.

The statement is an attack surface too. Here it comes from a curated database rather than from the claimant, and Comparator, the sandboxed judge, checks the candidate against the curated form.

Why the checker must not see the front end

The export rung took most of the lecture, and the phrasing that matters is that the re-checking version has no access to the front end. One checker is the official kernel itself, run standalone on an exported development, which sounds circular and is not: what is isolated is not the type theory but everything wrapped around it . A proof assistant is mostly convenience infrastructure. It processes files in parallel, recompiles incrementally, imports modules and reuses their compiled forms. All of it can have bugs, and a bug there can produce a check mark for a development a cold run would reject.

The second thing it removes is interference from metaprograms, which run at elaboration time and can corrupt memory or send terms to the kernel directly. A legitimate feature becomes an attack surface: there are options that switch off particular kernel checks, used when a definitional check is too slow to sit through, and metaprogramming can set them. Nothing in an export can reach them, because the export is a term and the standalone checker has no elaborator .

Comparing the checkers, and two very different failures

Several independent checkers now exist, written by different people, and their results are published side by side in the project's public arena. The columns encode a judgement about what a proof checker is for.

Accepts the benchmarks that should be acceptedThe checker rejects something that is in fact trueNot a disaster. It is visible and can be fixed or worked around.
Rejects what should be rejectedThe checker accepts something that should have been refusedA disaster, in the lecturer's own word.

Memory usedCorrect but does not fitSame. The resource columns keep the comparison honest about cost.

Key idea For a proof checker, soundness errors and completeness errors are not comparable failures. A checker that refuses a true development has annoyed you, and you will find out immediately, because the thing you know to be provable did not go through. A checker that accepts a development it should have refused has handed you a result that looks finished, and nobody has any reason to look at it again. The first is recoverable; the second is the failure the whole construction exists to prevent.

The benchmark set is not arbitrary: its weightiest entries come from Mathlib, because a checker exercised only on small examples has not been exercised. One column the table cannot show is the one he cared about most. What is wanted from a second checker is a decorrelated verdict rather than merely a second one, and some implementations are optimised rewrites on top of an existing checker: much faster, but sharing a code base, so a bug in the shared part appears in both.

When the attack is in the statement

The last rung addresses what the others cannot touch. Suppose the proof really is checked, by three independent checkers, on an export. The claimant can still cheat by proving a different theorem from the one you think you are reading, with invisible Unicode characters in identifiers or names chosen so that a reader believes a definition says something it does not. Nothing is wrong with the proof. The statement is not the statement. Lean cannot check whether an autoformalisation was faithful, and guarantees only that the proof proves exactly the statement written down . The answer is to stop taking statements from claimants: Formal Conjectures, maintained at Google DeepMind, is a curated collection of human-verified formal statements of open problems, so the question changes to whether the proof proves the curated version.

Pointing a model at the kernel

Comparator is the referee, a sandboxed judge for submitted proofs that exports the development and runs the official kernel and the independent ones over it, which is what defeats a metaprogramming exploit . The good case comes first: a challenge asks for a proof of False, a candidate tries to smuggle one past the official kernel with a trick exploiting a missing check, and Comparator rejects it, because once the proof is exported nanoda and lean4lean reject it too . Then the case that made the point. On 25 July a model constructed a proof of False that exploited a bug in the official kernel and a completely different bug in nanoda at the same time . Independent checkers are a real obstacle, and not an insurmountable one.

Getting useful results took a great deal of prompt engineering, most of it about what not to attack: exploits relying on unsafe language features, such as an unsafe cast that reinterprets a floating point value to obtain a proof of falsity, were out of scope because export and comparison already catch them, and so were bugs already known. The prompt is a map of the attack surface the existing defences do not cover.

Worth noticing With a model that has not been through the usual safety training, the persona that takes over when you ask it to hunt for bugs is a hacker persona picked up in pre-training. Told that further checks would be added, it reacted as though slighted and announced it would stop explaining what it was doing. He reported drily that it had given up two days earlier.

Verified kernels, and why several of them

Finishing a verified checker, and the project he wants finished is lean4lean, does not settle the question: verifying the checker leaves the runtime underneath it, and a bug there can be driven to corrupt memory and carry a bogus proof forward whatever has been proved about the logic. Running several checkers gives something weaker and more useful than a proof, the claim that at least one verdict is honest, which forces an attacker to hold two unrelated pieces of machinery wrong at once. Once a verified kernel exists, a model can be asked to translate it into another language, and then another, each translation inheriting the same proved logic and a completely different runtime with its own memory layout. He judged an exploit against all of them at once extremely hard, because exploits do not transfer between runtimes even when the logic is identical. It inverts the usual worry about models writing code: the model manufactures the diversity that makes the trusted computing base hard to attack, and each kernel can be checked against the others. The rest of the programme is concrete : routine build commands that run a development past Comparator and the external kernels, harder kernel invariants, and funding for anyone willing to write a new kernel.
