Lambda-Superposition

Jasmin Blanchette (LMU Munich), the third lecture of his series on provers and solvers, from the deck Provers and Solvers, Lecture 3: λ-Superposition, which he credits as partly based on slides by Alexander Bentkamp. No recording of this session is available, so this section follows the deck throughout.

The two preceding sections built first-order superposition: saturation with a given clause algorithm, redundancy and the deletion it licenses, and the superposition rule itself with its term ordering and literal selection. This lecture keeps every one of those pieces and asks what has to change when the input is higher-order, so that terms may contain lambda abstractions, a variable may stand for a function and be applied to arguments, and a symbol may appear partially applied. The answer is lambda-superposition, developed by Blanchette with Alexander Bentkamp, Sophie Tourret, Petar Vukmirović and Uwe Waldmann. The deck is organised around the three places where the first-order machinery breaks: the term ordering, unification, and Booleans.

What a first-order encoding loses

The motivating slide is a proof assistant with a goal open in it . The goal is that the sum of i² + 2i + 1 for i from 1 to n equals the sum of i², plus the sum of 2i, plus the sum of 1. The user presses a button marked Find Proof and expects an automatic prover to close it.

One way to get a first-order prover to attempt this is to translate the goal into first-order logic. The next slide, titled Lost in Translation, shows what comes out .

sum(1, n, C(B(plus, S(B(plus, C(power, 2)), app(times, 2))), 1))
= app(app(plus, app(app(plus, sum(1, n, C(power, 2))), sum(1, n, app(times, 2)))), sum(1, n, K(1)))

Every lambda abstraction has been compiled away into the combinators B, C, K and S, and every application has become an occurrence of a binary symbol app. The result is genuinely first-order and a first-order prover will accept it, but the structure that a human proof would exploit has dissolved into combinator plumbing. The slide makes no argument beyond putting the two formulas one above the other, and none is needed.

Design goal A sound, complete, graceful generalization of first-order superposition . Graceful is the load-bearing word: on input that happens to be first-order, the calculus should collapse back into ordinary superposition and impose no extra cost. The family picture on the opening slides places lambda-superposition directly above superposition, in the same relation that superposition bears to ordered resolution, with a ground version of each sitting underneath .

The logic and its clauses

A signature declares atomic types, such as bool and nat, and symbols with their types, such as 1, · and gcd. Terms are built by four rules : a variable declared with type τ is a term of type τ; a symbol declared with type τ is a term of type τ; if t has type τ then the abstraction λx : σ. t has type σ → τ; and if t has type σ → τ and t' has type σ then the application t t' has type τ. Formulas are simply terms of type bool.

Two consequences of that grammar are exactly what first-order syntax rules out. Application is a term former in its own right rather than something fixed by the shape of an atom, so the same symbol can occur applied to one argument, applied to two, and not applied at all, all within a single term. The deck's example list makes the point with p f (f a) (f a b) , in which f is passed as an argument and also applied to one and to two arguments. And abstraction lets a term bind its own variable, as in λx. λy. g y x.

Terms are then identified up to three rules . Alpha allows bound variables to be renamed, so g (λx. f x x) and g (λz. f z z) are the same term. Beta says (λx. t(x)) u = t(u), so (λx. f x x) a is f a a. Eta says (λx. t x) = t when x is not free in t, so g (f a a) and g (λy. f a a y) are the same term. Everything the calculus does happens modulo that equivalence rather than over syntax trees, which is already a departure from the first-order setting, where syntactic identity is the only identity there is.

The clause language, by contrast, is unchanged . An atom is an equation t₁ = t₂ viewed as an unordered pair. A literal is an equation or a disequation. A clause is a multiset of literals, and the empty clause is written ⊥. Two footnotes on that slide matter later: the connectives ⊤, ∧, negation, ∀ and ∃ may appear inside terms, and the free variables of a clause are read universally.

What carries over The saturation framework of the earlier sections survives intact. There is still an active set and a passive set, still a given clause algorithm, still redundancy-driven deletion, still a term ordering and a literal selection restricting which inferences fire, and a refutation still ends when ⊥ appears. Nothing in this lecture replaces that architecture. Every change is a repair to one of its components.

The running example, worked

The same summation goal comes back written in higher-order syntax . The goal becomes sum 1 n (λi. i² + 2i + 1) = sum 1 n (λi. i²) + sum 1 n (λi. 2i) + sum 1 n (λi. 1), and the distributivity lemma available to the prover becomes sum 1 n (λi. f i + g i) = sum 1 n (λi. f i) + sum 1 n (λi. g i), with f and g universally quantified over functions. Compared with the combinator encoding these are readable, and more to the point the lemma now looks like something that could match the goal. Note where the difficulty sits: f i is a variable applied to an argument, underneath a binder.

Two clauses. The lemma is a positive equation, sum 1 n (λi. f i + g i) = sum 1 n (λi. f i) + sum 1 n (λi. g i), with f and g free and therefore universally quantified. The negated goal is a disequation, sum 1 n (λi. i² + 2i + 1) ≠ sum 1 n (λi. i²) + sum 1 n (λi. 2i) + sum 1 n (λi. 1) .

Superposition needs a subterm of one clause that the other clause's equation can rewrite. The lemma is used from right to left here, so the subterm to match is sum 1 n (λi. i²) + sum 1 n (λi. 2i), the first two summands on the right of the disequation.

Match sum 1 n (λi. f i) + sum 1 n (λi. g i) against it. The solution is {f ↦ λi. i², g ↦ λi. 2i}. This is where higher-order unification is doing real work: the unifier has to invent lambda terms as the values of f and g, and the match only succeeds modulo beta, since (λi. i²) i has to be recognised as i².

Replacing the matched subterm by the left side of the lemma under that substitution yields sum 1 n (λi. i² + 2i + 1) ≠ sum 1 n (λi. i² + 2i) + sum 1 n (λi. 1) . Two summands have been merged into one.

The same lemma applies again to the new clause, this time with {f ↦ λi. i² + 2i, g ↦ λi. 1}. The value of g is a constant function, which is precisely what the combinator encoding had to spell out as K 1. The result is sum 1 n (λi. i² + 2i + 1) ≠ sum 1 n (λi. i² + 2i + 1) .

The two sides of the disequation are now the same term, so the literal is false under every interpretation and can be struck out by equality resolution, the rule from the previous section. Nothing is left, and the clause set contains ⊥ . One lemma, used twice, plus one clean-up step.

Challenge 1: the term ordering

The deck names three design challenges , and the ordering is the first. First-order superposition requires two properties of its term ordering . A subterm must be smaller than the whole term, so c < g(c) < f(g(c)). And placing two terms in the same context must preserve their orientation, so if b > a then f(b, c) > f(a, c). In higher-order logic the two cannot both hold.

The counterexample is short . Start from (λy. f (λz. z)) > (λz. z), which the subterm requirement forces, since the identity function occurs inside the term on the left. Now place both terms in the same context, namely apply each of them to the argument g (f (λz. z)). On the left the bound variable y does not occur in the body, so beta reduction throws the argument away and leaves f (λz. z). On the right the term is the identity, so beta reduction returns the argument itself, g (f (λz. z)). By the subterm requirement the second of these is the larger. The orientation has flipped, and the deck marks the clash with a lightning bolt.

The repair has three parts . Compatibility with contexts is weakened, so that only good contexts are required to preserve orientation. The main superposition rule is then restricted to act only on good contexts. And because that restriction removes inferences the calculus needs, an extra rule compensates: if a clause C ∨ t = t' is in the clause set and t and t' are functions, add the clause C ∨ t x = t' x, with x fresh.

The deck does not give the full definition of a good context. What it does give is one context that is not good, and a worked example built around it . Take the clause set containing g = f and g a ≠ f a, which is plainly unsatisfiable. The obvious inference rewrites the g inside g a using g = f, giving f a ≠ f a, which equality resolution closes at once. That inference is disallowed, and the slide says why: the hole sits in the function position of an application, and [ ] a is not a good context .

The extra rule supplies the missing route. Applied to g = f it produces g x = f x . Now the superposition step rewrites the whole left-hand side g a rather than a symbol buried in its function position, so the context is empty and therefore good, and the same conclusion f a ≠ f a is reached legitimately, followed by ⊥ . The extra rule is argument congruence, and its soundness is one line: if g and f denote the same function in an interpretation, then g a and f a denote the same value for every a .

Challenge 1bis: keeping the extra rule from deleting itself

The extra rule immediately upsets the redundancy criterion inherited from the first-order theory . In first-order logic a ground clause C is redundant with respect to a ground clause set if some clauses of that set, all smaller than C, together entail it. But g = f entails g x = f x and is smaller than it, so the conclusion of the extra rule is redundant with respect to its own premise. A prover applying the criterion literally would delete the conclusion as soon as it appeared, and would never obtain the clause the previous section's example depends on.

The fix is to weaken the notion of entailment used inside the criterion . The deck pins the intended behaviour down by two examples rather than by a definition: b = a does make f b = f a redundant, and g = f does not make g a = f a redundant. The following slide indicates how the distinction is drawn, by annotating the symbols with the number of arguments they carry, so that the second pair is read as g₀ = f₀ against g₁ a₀ = f₁ a₀ . Under that reading a symbol applied to one argument is a different symbol from the same symbol applied to none, so the equation between the unapplied versions says nothing about the applied ones. Rewriting an argument, as in the first example, is unaffected.

Lifting to clauses with variables is then the usual construction : a non-ground clause C is redundant with respect to a non-ground clause set F if every ground instance of C is redundant with respect to the ground instances of F. The two examples track the ground ones. b x = a makes f (b x) = f a redundant, while g = f does not make g x = f x redundant, which is exactly the outcome the extra rule needs.

With a workable criterion in place, the deck gives one redundancy-based simplification specific to higher-order clauses, called argument pruning . If a clause contains an applied variable one of whose arguments is computable from the others, the argument carries no information and can be removed. Three instances appear on the slide.

ClausePruned toWhy the argument is redundant
y b b ≠ y a ay b ≠ y aThe second argument always repeats the first.
y a c ≠ y a by c ≠ y bThe first argument is the constant a at every occurrence.
y a c ≠ z b dy ≠ zEach variable always receives the same arguments, so all of them go.

Each pruned clause is more general than the one it came from and makes it redundant, so the original is deleted. This is the higher-order counterpart of the simplifications that keep a first-order prover's clause set from growing without bound.

Challenge 2: unification stops being unitary

In first-order logic a most general unifier always exists when a unifier exists at all, and it can be computed: unifying f(a, y) with f(x, b) gives {x ↦ a, y ↦ b}, and every other unifier is an instance of it . Three things go wrong at higher order .

  • Most general unifiers do not always exist. Unifying f (y a) with y (f a) yields infinitely many unifiers: {y ↦ λx. x}, {y ↦ λx. f x}, {y ↦ λx. f (f x)}, and so on. Each one works, and none is an instance of another. Check the second: both sides become f (f a).
  • Unification is undecidable. There is no procedure that always tells you whether two higher-order terms are unifiable, so a prover cannot treat unification as a subroutine it calls and waits on.
  • Applied variables can hide positions where inferences should be made. A subterm that a rewriting step needs may not be present in the clause at all, and may appear only once a variable standing for a function has been instantiated.

Each has its own answer . For the first, the calculus uses a possibly infinite sequence of unifiers in place of a most general unifier, so an inference produces a sequence of conclusions rather than one. For the second, unification and inference are interweaved, so that the prover never blocks on a unification problem that may not terminate but keeps making progress elsewhere. For the third, there is a special fluid version of the main inference rule.

The fluid case is worth following. Take the clause set containing f a = c and h (y b) (y a) ≠ h (g (f b)) (g c) . It is unsatisfiable, and the refutation runs through the substitution {y ↦ λx. g (f x)}, which turns the second clause into h (g (f b)) (g (f a)) ≠ h (g (f b)) (g c) . Only now is there an f a for the first clause to rewrite, and rewriting it gives a disequation between two identical terms and hence ⊥ . Standard superposition inspects subterms that are actually present in its premises, so it never sees this position and never finds the proof.

The fluid rule makes the hidden position visible by lifting the equation into an unknown context. The deck writes the first premise as z (f a) = z c, where z is a fresh function variable standing for whatever surrounds the rewritten subterm, and then unifies z (f a) with the applied-variable subterm y a of the second premise . Higher-order unification answers {z ↦ g, y ↦ λx. g (f x)}. Substituting z c, that is g c, for that position produces h (g (f b)) (g c) ≠ h (g (f b)) (g c), which equality resolution closes . The substitution that a human would have had to guess is instead delivered by the unifier.

Challenge 3: Boolean expressions inside terms

In first-order logic terms and formulas are syntactically distinct, and clausification is a straightforward preprocessing pass that works on the outer skeleton of a formula . Higher-order logic makes formulas a special case of terms, and two problems follow . Formulas can appear nested inside terms, including underneath lambdas, as in λx. if ∃y. p x y then a else b. And as a consequence, clausification cannot be carried out entirely in preprocessing, because inferences and substitutions can create Boolean structure that was not there when the input was read. The response is to add dedicated inference rules that clausify dynamically, during saturation rather than before it .

Soundness, completeness, and fairness over streams

Soundness is the easy direction and the deck says so : the inference rules are straightforward to justify, and the one that needed comment, the extra rule, follows from argument congruence in the semantics. Completeness is stated in the familiar refutational form : if the clause set is initially unsatisfiable and inferences are performed fairly, then the clause set will eventually contain ⊥.

Read the asterisk The deck attaches a footnote to the word unsatisfiable: unsatisfiable with respect to the so-called Henkin semantics . In Henkin semantics the domain interpreting a function type need not contain every function from one domain to another, only enough of them for the language to make sense. It is the standard setting in which higher-order logic admits a complete proof calculus at all, and the deck flags it without elaborating further.

The word fairly is where the unification story returns. The standard saturation loop of the earlier sections has two compartments, passive and active, and fairness means that every clause put into passive eventually becomes the given clause . That is not enough here, because an inference no longer yields a clause. It yields a sequence of them, one per unifier, and the sequence may be infinite. The generalized loop therefore has a third compartment ahead of passive, holding those sequences as streams . The loop repeatedly pulls one more element off some stream and drops it into passive, alongside its ordinary work of moving clauses from passive into active.

Fairness therefore acquires a second obligation. Every clause in passive must still eventually become active, but in addition every stream must eventually be advanced. A prover that kept drawing from one stream forever would never idle and would still be unfair, because the conclusion that refutes the clause set might be waiting in a stream it never touched. That is the sense in which the answer to Challenge 2 is not only a change of rule but a change to the loop the earlier sections described.

The four changes in one paragraph

Lambda-superposition keeps the clause language, the saturation loop and the inference shapes of first-order superposition, and modifies four things. The term ordering only has to respect contexts that are good, with argument congruence added as a separate rule to recover the inferences that restriction costs. The redundancy criterion uses a weaker entailment, so that argument congruence does not delete its own conclusion. Unification returns a possibly infinite sequence of unifiers rather than one, is interweaved with inference rather than run to completion, and is supplemented by a fluid rule that reaches positions hidden behind applied variables. And clausification of Boolean subterms happens during saturation rather than before it. The calculus is sound, and refutationally complete with respect to Henkin semantics under a fairness condition that now covers streams as well as clauses.
