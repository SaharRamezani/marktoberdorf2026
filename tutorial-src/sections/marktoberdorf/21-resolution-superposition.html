Provers and Solvers: Saturation and Resolution

Lecture by Jasmin Blanchette (LMU Munich), the first of four on provers and solvers. This section follows his deck Provers and Solvers, Lecture 1: Resolution together with the recording of the session. The deck is built up overlay by overlay, so a page reference names the page on which the slide is complete.

This series fits the rest of the school closely: Blanchette works on interactive theorem proving, on automated provers and solvers, and on making the two work together. That combination shapes the first lecture. Before any calculus appears, he says what an automatic prover is actually for and why it has not been made redundant by generative models. Then he introduces saturation, the proof-search framework that all four lectures share, and the given clause algorithm that turns the framework into something you can run. The rest of the lecture builds resolution up in three passes: the ground case first, where clauses carry no variables, then an ordering restriction that stops the ground calculus from drowning, then variables, unification and the lifting of the whole theory to first-order clauses.

What the prover is for

Blanchette comes to automated deduction from the interactive side. In a proof assistant the user writes the proof and the system checks it step by step, and the ambition is that a step the user considers obvious should not have to be written out at all. Instead the assistant passes the step to an automatic theorem prover running as a back end. The user clicks a button, and a second or two later the step is either discharged or reported as not proved.

The example he opened with is an elementary fact about divisibility and greatest common divisors : if d divides a·b, and A = gcd(d, a), and B = gcd(d, b), then d divides A·B. The deck puts it in a mock proof assistant window under a "Find Proof" button, and the next page shows the button having produced a tick. The same formula returns later as a specimen of what a first-order problem looks like . The point is that this is a small, purely first-order fact that a mathematician would call obvious and would never write out, and whose manual proof inside a proof assistant is tedious out of all proportion to its interest. Until a few years ago that tedium was simply part of the job. How recently depends on the system: he noted that users of Rocq were still doing proofs of this kind by hand around five years ago.

Program verification is the other large consumer of exactly the same technology, and he acknowledged it, but the interactive setting is where his own interest comes from and it is the setting the lectures keep in view.

Why not just ask a model

He raised the objection himself, and answered it before starting on the technical material. Against a generative model, an automatic prover has four concrete advantages.

  • It is faster on the things it is good at. Its range is relatively simple formulas, but "relatively simple" here often means harder than a human can dispatch in a few minutes.
  • It is far less energy hungry.
  • It runs on a laptop, with no service to call.
  • A model can use it. The prover can serve as one proof step inside a larger, model-driven argument, which makes the relationship complementary rather than competitive.

He also turned the framing round. When he says AI in this argument he means generative AI, the kind behind current chat and image systems. But an automatic theorem prover is itself a form of AI, of the older, classical sort. His conclusion was measured rather than defensive: what follows is not obsoleted by generative AI, at least not yet, and it is something to use in collaboration with a model, or without one.

The remark about lecture two is worth pausing on, because it says something about the state of the field. Almost no pure resolution provers are left; almost all of them are superposition provers. If you have heard of Vampire or E, those are superposition provers, which can still perform resolution inferences when that is what a problem calls for. Resolution is taught first because it is the simpler calculus and because superposition is best understood as a generalisation of it, not because it is what you would reach for today.

Saturation, the shared framework

All four lectures are based on saturation, so that is where the technical content starts . The setting is a proof goal: a set of axioms or hypotheses F1 to Fn, and a conjecture G to be proved from them.

  1. Take F1, ..., Fn and ¬G, the negation of the goal.
  2. Put them all into a normal form. This yields a set of formulas, call it F.
  3. Repeat:
    1. apply an inference rule to members of F, and add the conclusion to F;
    2. delete from F any formula that has become redundant.
  4. Stop when ⊥ (falsity) has been derived.

The move in step 1 is the one that makes the whole thing a search for a contradiction rather than a search for a proof. You do not start from the axioms and try to reach the goal. You start from the axioms together with the assumption that the goal is false, and try to reach absurdity. In classical logic, deriving ⊥ from that set establishes the goal: if the axioms plus ¬G are jointly unsatisfiable, then every model of the axioms is a model of G. This is why saturation provers are called refutational, and why the answer they give you is not a proof of G directly but a refutation of its negation.

Definition Saturation. A proof procedure that maintains a growing set of formulas, closes it under the inference rules of a calculus, and deletes formulas that have become redundant, until falsity appears. The set is "saturated" when every inference between formulas in it has already been performed, or made unnecessary by deletion.

Redundancy, and why deletion complicates the theory

Step 3b is the one that is easy to skip past and should not be. A saturation prover does not only add formulas. If it notices that some formula cannot help, typically because it now has a better formula in hand, it throws that formula away. Without deletion the set of derived formulas grows without limit and the prover drowns in its own conclusions.

The example on the slide itself is the simplest possible one . Suppose you have derived P. Then P ∨ Q becomes redundant, in most calculi he knows of, because P is a strictly better formula: it tells you more.

"P could be, the sky is blue. And P or Q is, the sky is blue or it is raining. Which one of the two is more informative? The first, the sky is blue, because then you know for sure the sky is blue."

Jasmin Blanchette, on redundancy

Anything derivable with the help of the disjunction is derivable from P alone, so nothing is lost by discarding it, and a formula that would otherwise have generated inferences of its own is removed from the search space.

Careful Because formulas can be removed as well as added, saturation is not monotone. The set F does not simply grow over time. Blanchette flagged this explicitly as one of the reasons saturation is tricky to reason about: a proof of completeness cannot argue that whatever was once derivable stays available, since the prover may have deleted it. The redundancy criterion has to be strong enough to guarantee that deletion never removes anything the refutation actually needed.

The given clause algorithm

The saturation loop as stated says "apply an inference rule" without saying which one, or to what. Left like that it is a specification, not a program: you could perform one inference, then another, in any order at all, and re-do inferences you have already done. The organisation used in practice is the given clause algorithm, which the deck presents as an animated picture of two boxes labelled passive and active , and it works by splitting the formulas into those two sets.

The normalised axioms and the negated goal all begin in the passive set. The active set is empty. Active means "already processed": every formula in it has already had all its inferences with all the other active formulas performed.

Pick one formula out of passive. This is the given clause for this turn. Which one you pick is a heuristic choice, and it is where much of a real prover's cleverness lives.

Perform every possible inference between the given clause and the members of the active set. If two active formulas can be combined with it, do both. Each conclusion is a child of the given clause.

The children go into the passive set, where they wait their turn like everything else. They are not immediately usable as inference partners, which is exactly what keeps the bookkeeping finite: inferences only ever happen between the given clause and active formulas.

Move the given clause itself from passive to active. It has now been fully processed against everything active, so the invariant is restored, and the loop can begin again.

If ⊥ is derived, stop: the goal is proved. Otherwise repeat. The aim is to move everything from passive to active, which the prover may well not manage in finite time.

That last point needs the fairness condition, which Blanchette stated informally but clearly. You may not be able to empty the passive set in finite time, but you can always arrange to empty it in the limit, provided you select formulas fairly: no formula may be left sitting in passive forever. If selection is fair, every formula is eventually chosen as the given clause, and so every possible inference is eventually performed. Without fairness the prover can starve exactly the formula the refutation needed, and then it will search forever without finding a proof that exists.

The logic underneath

With the loop in place the lecture goes back to first principles and fixes the logic the calculus works over . A signature supplies function symbols, such as 1, multiplication and gcd, and predicate symbols, such as equality, divisibility and prime. A variable is a term, and so is f(t1, ..., tn) for a function symbol f and terms ti. A formula is a predicate symbol applied to terms, or one of ⊥, ⊤, ¬F, F ∧ G, F ∨ G, F → G, ∀x. F and ∃x. F. Besides the gcd statement, the deck's example is a puzzle : Johanna likes everything that is food; anything that someone eats and is not killed by it is food; Bill eats peanuts and Bill is alive; nothing alive was killed by anything; therefore Johanna likes peanuts. He noted that the implication is valid as it stands, and that the example uses only nullary function symbols, that is, constants.

An interpretation J consists of a nonempty domain D, a function on D for each function symbol and a relation on D for each predicate symbol, lifted compositionally to ground terms, meaning that a truth value is assigned to ground formulas . Nothing is said about variables: the clausal fragment makes them implicitly universal, and they are dealt with only in the last third of the lecture, by grounding rather than by variable assignments.

Clauses and clause normal form

Resolution does not operate on formulas of that generality but on clauses, and the clausal fragment is deliberately narrow . An atom is a predicate symbol applied to terms; a literal is an atom or a negated atom; a clause is a disjunction L1 ∨ ... ∨ Ln of literals, and the case n = 0 is the empty clause, written ⊥. There is no ⊤, no conjunction, no implication and no quantifier, and the variables that occur are read as universally quantified so that no prefix is written . One convention matters later: a clause is a multiset of literals rather than a set, so duplicates are kept rather than merged, a detail that starts to count once equality reasoning arrives.

Any formula can be turned into clauses by a fixed procedure .

  1. Rewrite every implication F → G to ¬F ∨ G.
  2. Push negations inwards using De Morgan's laws, and eliminate double negations.
  3. Pull ∀ and ∃ outwards, to the front of the formula.
  4. Replace each existential quantifier with a Skolem function symbol: a fresh symbol applied to the universally quantified variables the existential sits under.
  5. Remove the universal quantifiers, which are now the only ones left, and so leave the variables universally implicit.
  6. Distribute ∨ inwards over ∧.

Each conjunct of the result is a clause.

The worked example is the syllogism . From (∀x. human(x) → mortal(x)) and human(anne) one wants mortal(anne), so the input to clausification is those two hypotheses together with the negated goal ¬mortal(anne). Step 1 turns the implication into ∀x. ¬human(x) ∨ mortal(x) and step 5 drops the quantifier, leaving three clauses: ¬human(x) ∨ mortal(x), human(anne), and ¬mortal(anne). Step 6 is the dangerous one in practice, since distributing naively can enlarge a formula badly, and the deck's reading list carries a reference on doing it properly.

The ground resolution rule

The rule is stated first for ground clauses, so that variables and unification can be dealt with separately later .

Rule Ground resolution. If the clauses C ∨ A and ¬A ∨ D are contained in F, then add the clause C ∨ D to F. Here A is an atom, and C and D are the remainders of the two clauses, either of which may be empty.

The premises agree on an atom and disagree on its sign, and the conclusion is what survives once the complementary literals cancel. If both premises are single literals, A and ¬A, the conclusion is ⊥. On the ground version of the syllogism the refutation is two steps long : ¬human(anne) ∨ mortal(anne) with human(anne) gives mortal(anne), and that with ¬mortal(anne) gives ⊥.

Soundness is proved by a two-row table on the truth value of A . If A is true then ¬A is false, so D must be true for the second premise to hold, and C ∨ D is true. If A is false then C must be true for the first premise to hold, and again C ∨ D is true. There is no third case, so a derivation of ⊥ genuinely certifies that the input was unsatisfiable, which is what makes the refutational reading of step 1 legitimate.

No branching, until lecture four

One structural property is worth fixing in mind now, because it distinguishes this family of calculi from the case analysis that SMT solvers perform. When resolution finishes a proof, that is the whole proof. There is no branching and there are no cases to discharge separately: a single derivation of ⊥ from the initial set is the entire argument. The same holds for superposition. Branching arrives only in the fourth lecture, where case splitting is added. There the prover does split into multiple cases, and ⊥ has to be derived separately in each of them before the goal counts as proved. Until then, every loop in the diagram above works on one undivided set of formulas.

Completeness, and the model that proves it

Completeness is the converse of soundness and it is where fairness earns its keep : if the ground clause set F is unsatisfiable to begin with and inferences are performed fairly, then F will eventually contain ⊥. The proof is in two moves. Fairness gives that the limit of the run is saturated, meaning every available inference has been performed. A separate result, called static completeness, then says that a saturated unsatisfiable set already contains ⊥.

Key idea The split turns a statement about a run of a prover into a statement about a single set of clauses, so a new calculus need only re-prove static completeness and inherits the rest. Blanchette co-authored the paper the deck cites for the general version .

Static completeness is proved in contrapositive form : a saturated set that does not contain ⊥ has a model. The model the deck builds is a term model , in which every term denotes itself, so that it is completely described by the set of ground atoms it makes true: {p(f(a))} makes p(f(a)) true and everything else false.

It is built by walking through the clauses in increasing order under an ordering on clauses, carrying a set of true atoms along . At each clause there are three cases: if it is already true, nothing happens; if it is false and its maximal literal is a positive atom, that atom is added and the clause is said to produce it; if it is false and its maximal literal is negative, the construction is stuck. The deck's run reaches the stuck case on the last of five clauses.

ClauseModel so farAddedWhy

¬q ∨ t ∨ s{q, r}{t}t is maximal
¬q ∨ ¬t ∨ s{q, r, t}{}false, and its maximal literal is negative

The last row is the whole argument in miniature. The clause is false in {q, r, t} and its maximal literal ¬t is negative, so nothing can be added. But the set is saturated, so the inference between it and the clause that produced t has already been performed: resolving ¬q ∨ t ∨ s with ¬q ∨ ¬t ∨ s on t yields ¬q ∨ ¬q ∨ s ∨ s, which is false in the same model and smaller in the ordering . That contradicts the choice of a smallest false clause, so the stuck case never arises.

The doubled literals are not a slip: the multiset convention accounts for the repetition, and the resolvent is still smaller than its premise because the maximal literal has gone. The lecture works with the single resolution rule throughout and adds no rule for collapsing repeated literals. The ordering on clauses is never defined : the slide says only that it is tricky to state precisely and invites the question offline. What the argument uses is visible in the table, namely that deleting a maximal literal produces a smaller clause, and that the redundancy criterion below refers to the same order.

Ordering the inferences

Unrestricted ground resolution is complete and also hopeless. The demonstration is the clause set a ∨ b ∨ c ∨ d ∨ e together with the five units ¬a, ¬b, ¬c, ¬d and ¬e . Any literal of the long clause may be cancelled against its unit, in any order, so the prover generates all five four-literal clauses, then all ten three-literal ones, then all ten two-literal ones, then the units and finally ⊥: the entire powerset of the five atoms. A good run is five inferences , cancelling one literal at a time down a single chain. The difference is not which inferences exist but which ones the prover is permitted to make.

Rule Ordered ground resolution. If C ∨ A and ¬A ∨ D are contained in F, and A is maximal in C ∨ A, and ¬A is maximal in ¬A ∨ D, then add C ∨ D to F.

That is the whole change . With an ordering in which e is the largest atom, the only literal of the long clause that may be resolved away is e, so the good run is the only run. Soundness needs no new argument, the restricted rule being a special case of the unrestricted one , and completeness survives because the model construction already looked only at maximal literals . That is the payoff of proving completeness by building a model rather than by manipulating derivations.

Redundancy stated precisely, and subsumption

The informal notion of a useless clause from the start of the lecture now gets a definition .

Definition Ground redundancy. A ground clause C is redundant with respect to a ground set F if there are clauses C1, ..., Cn in F that are all smaller than C in the clause ordering and that together entail C.

Both halves matter: entailment alone would let the prover trade a clause for larger ones, and smallness alone says nothing. The two examples given are the unit clause a, which makes a ∨ b redundant, and the pair b ∨ a and ¬a ∨ c, which together make b ∨ c redundant. The first is subsumption, the case in which one clause is contained in another; the second shows the criterion is strictly more general. The deck then asks why completeness still goes through . The answer is that the model construction turns on a smallest false clause, and a redundant clause cannot be one: the smaller clauses that entail it are all true at that point, and true premises cannot entail a false conclusion.

Redundancy alone rescues the bad scenario, with no ordering at all . Resolve the long clause with ¬e and the child a ∨ b ∨ c ∨ d subsumes its own parent, which is deleted; the next inference deletes the next parent, and so on. The set never grows, it shrinks by a literal per step until a and then ⊥ appear, at which point everything else is redundant with respect to ⊥. Ordering and redundancy attack the same blow-up from two directions, and a real prover uses both.

He also placed the calculus next to SAT solving . Conflict-driven clause learning is orders of magnitude more efficient than ground resolution, so nobody would build a SAT solver this way. What ground resolution has, and CDCL does not, is a generalisation to clauses with variables that remains competitive, and that generalisation is the rest of the lecture.

Variables, unification and the nonground rule

The intended meaning of a clause C(x) with a variable in it is its grounding , the set ground(C(x)) of all its variable-free instances: given function symbols a, b and f, that is C(a), C(b), C(f(a)), C(f(b)), C(f(f(a))) and so on without end. A prover cannot enumerate that, and the point of the nonground calculus is to do in one inference what the ground calculus would need infinitely many for.

Rule Nonground resolution. If C ∨ A and ¬A' ∨ D are contained in F, and the substitution σ is the most general unifier of the atoms A and A', then add σ(C ∨ D) to F.

The two atoms no longer have to be identical . They have to be unifiable, which means only that some substitution makes them equal, and the substitution applied to the conclusion is the most general such one, of which every other unifier is an instance. Using the most general unifier is what keeps the step from committing to anything the problem did not force.

The worked example is the one on the deck's title page, spelled out . Take C(y) ∨ p(a, y) and ¬p(x, b) ∨ D(x). The two atoms differ, but σ = {x ↦ a, y ↦ b} unifies them at p(a, b), so under σ the premises read C(b) ∨ p(a, b) and ¬p(a, b) ∨ D(a), and the conclusion is σ(C(y) ∨ D(x)), that is C(b) ∨ D(a). The premises deliberately use different variable names, since each clause quantifies its own variables and the two must be renamed apart before unification. The syllogism is then redone on the nonground clauses, just as it stands : human(x) unifies with human(anne) under {x ↦ anne}, giving mortal(anne), and one more step gives ⊥. No instance had to be guessed in advance.

Lifting the theory

Every ground result now has to be recovered, and the deck does it uniformly by pushing each statement through the grounding map. A nonground clause C is redundant with respect to F when every clause in ground(C) is redundant with respect to ground(F) , and subsumption can be seen at the general level: a(x) makes a(x) ∨ b(x) redundant. Soundness is phrased the same way , completeness keeps its two-move shape , and static completeness is reduced to the ground case : show that ground(F) is saturated whenever F is, apply the ground result to put ⊥ into ground(F), and conclude that ⊥ is in F, since the only clause whose grounding contains ⊥ is ⊥ itself.

The step that needs care is that ground(F) really is saturated . The illustration takes F to be p(x) ∨ q(x) and ¬q(y). One nonground inference produces p(x), after which F is saturated. The grounding contains p(a) ∨ q(a) with ¬q(a) and so on for every ground term, and those ground resolvents are p(a), p(b), p(c), each an instance of p(x). The single nonground conclusion covers the whole infinite family: nonground inferences over-approximate ground ones, and that is what the reduction needs.

Where the material comes from

The deck closes on three references : Bachmair and Ganzinger's chapter "Resolution Theorem Proving" in the Handbook of Automated Reasoning, the standard account of ordered resolution with redundancy and the source of the argument above; Nonnengart and Weidenbach's "Computing Small Clause Normal Forms", in the same handbook, on clausification as it is really implemented; and "A Comprehensive Framework for Saturation Theorem Proving" by Uwe Waldmann, Sophie Tourret, Simon Robillard and Blanchette, in the Journal of Automated Reasoning in 2022, the general form of the fairness plus static completeness separation that the later lectures reuse.
