P4 and P4-SpecTec

Sukyoung Ryu (KAIST), from the deck Mechanized Specifications (3): P4 and P4-SpecTec, 20 August 2026. There is no recording of this lecture, so these notes follow the slides alone.

The first two lectures of this series each started from a document. JavaScript has ECMA-262, a large and awkward standard but a standard all the same, and WebAssembly has a specification written by people who wanted it to be formal. P4 is the case where that starting point is missing. Its official specification is informal prose that the deck describes as not systematic, its formalizations are years out of date, and the thing everyone actually treats as the definition of the language is a compiler written in C++. This lecture is about what mechanization looks like when there is no authoritative document to mechanize, and about one concrete payoff: a way to measure whether a negative test suite has covered the type errors a language can have, and a fuzzer that pushes that measure up.

Where this sits in the series

This is the third of four lectures on mechanized specifications for languages used in industry: JavaScript and ESMeta, WebAssembly and SpecTec, P4 and P4-SpecTec, then other programming languages together with non-PL software . Sections 15 and 16 cover the first two, so the ESMeta pipeline and the SpecTec meta-language are not re-derived here. What matters for this lecture is the shape of the argument they established. Take the definition of a language, express it once in a machine-readable meta-language, and generate the artifacts that used to be maintained separately by hand.

The WebAssembly effort is used as the template, and the deck reprints its timeline as a reminder of how long adoption takes : a Dagstuhl seminar in March 2023, a first presentation to the WebAssembly Community Group in October 2023, the PLDI paper and a conditional acceptance of the adoption plan in June 2024, official acceptance of that plan in March 2025, WebAssembly 3.0 in September 2025, and an ASE paper in November 2025 that received an ACM SIGSOFT Distinguished Paper Award. Roughly two and a half years from a research idea to a standards body committing to it.

The deck opens with two pointers on research craft, Simon Peyton Jones on how to write a great research paper and a PLMW talk from ICFP 2022 hosted on Derek Dreyer's page . Both slides carry only the link, so there is nothing further to report about what was said around them.

The P4 material itself is structured around three questions that the deck returns to twice as section dividers : what mechanization is and why it is needed, how the P4 specification is mechanized, and what the benefits are. The work is P4-SpecTec, presented by Jaehyun Lee, Seokhun Jeong and Sukyoung Ryu at HATRA 2025 , with the negative testing half published at FSE 2026.

What P4 is

P4 stands for Programming Protocol-independent Packet Processors. It is a domain specific language for programming packet-processing devices, switches being the usual example, and a P4 program specifies how such a device parses, modifies and emits a network packet .

The point of the language is in the word protocol-independent. A traditional switch has its supported protocols burned into the hardware: it knows what an Ethernet frame is, what an IP header looks like, and what to do with them, and if you want it to understand something new you wait for the next generation of the chip. P4 inverts that. The device provides a programmable pipeline, and the P4 program tells it what the headers are, how to pull them out of the incoming bytes, what to do with the fields it finds, and how to write a packet back out. The protocols become part of the program rather than part of the silicon.

For the purposes of this lecture two properties of P4 matter more than the networking . Its syntax and semantics are C-like, so nothing about the language itself is exotic to a reader who knows an imperative language, and it is statically typed. The static type system is what the rest of the lecture is about. Types in P4 carry width information, so bit<8> is an eight-bit unsigned value and int<8> an eight-bit signed one, and the type rules have to reason about those widths as well as about the shape of the terms.

Four representations, and why they drift apart

Viewed from a programming languages perspective, P4 exists in four representations at once . There is the official specification, written in informal prose. There are implementations, principally p4c, the reference compiler written in C++. There are formalizations, in precise mathematical notation. And there are tests, which are input-output examples.

The question the deck puts next is whether those four are actually in sync . In principle yes; in practice no, because they are maintained by different parties and evolve at different paces. The concrete illustration is generic types . The official specification introduced them in May 2021. The implementation side did not settle generics together with type inference until a patch in March 2025. The formalizations are recorded as still pre-2021, the slide adding that the most recent of them is based on 2024. One feature, three representations, and three different answers to the question of whether the language has it.

That leads to the question the whole project is answering : can a programming language evolve and keep its representations consistent at the same time? The proposed answer is to stop treating the four as peers and make one of them primary.

Definition The specification model is an unambiguous and complete definition of a language's syntax and semantics, for example a complete formalization or a definitional interpreter . It is the single source of truth underlying the other representations: the official specification is the model made verbose with prose, the implementation is the model realised in a programming language, and the tests are concrete examples drawn from it.

Definition A language mechanization framework provides a DSL, called the meta-language, for writing the specification model down, and automates the generation of the various backends from it . SpecTec, from lecture 2, is such a framework; P4-SpecTec is the one built for P4.

Why P4 is harder than WebAssembly

The WebAssembly work succeeded because the recipe had an ingredient that was already there . There was a standard, and that standard drove the design of the spec model: the formal reduction rules in the mechanized version correspond to something the standard already said. The mechanization was, in a real sense, a translation.

P4 offers nothing equivalent . Taking the four representations again and asking which of them could serve as the authority, the deck marks each one as unsuitable. The official specification is informal and not systematic. The implementation side is a nano-pass compiler, which is well engineered but is a compiler, not a definition. The formalism is incomplete and not up to date. The tests are incomplete. There is no artifact you can point at and say that this is what the language means.

Key idea For JavaScript the problem was that the specification was hard to read mechanically. For P4 the problem is that there is no specification worth reading mechanically. The mechanized model therefore cannot be extracted from a document, as ESMeta extracts one from ECMA-262. It has to be written, and then justified against the only thing the community currently trusts, which is the reference compiler and its test suite.

From declarative rules to algorithmic ones

The next question is whether the WebAssembly SpecTec meta-language can simply be reused . It has two properties that are attractive for any language. It uses declarative inference rules, which hide implementation-level details, and it enforces case analysis by disallowing nested control flow. That second constraint is worth looking at, because it turns out to matter later. Where an ordinary programming language would write a conditional inside a rule, the meta-language makes you split the rule in two:

not allowed                     required instead
-----------                     ----------------
LHS ~> RHS                      LHS ~> RHS          LHS ~> RHS
-- if cond:                     -- if cond:         -- if not cond:
     -- ... foo ...                  -- ... foo ...      -- ... bar ...
-- else:
     -- ... bar ...

The answer to the reuse question is a qualified no . Declarative inference rules are not directly executable. That is tolerable for WebAssembly's dynamic semantics, but it breaks down for static semantics, where the whole point is to obtain a type checker you can run. The example on the slide is the transitivity rule for subtyping:

rule Type_sub/trans:
  C ⊢ type_a <: type_c
  ----
  -- Type_sub: C ⊢ type_a <: type_b
  -- Type_sub: C ⊢ type_b <: type_c

Read as a declaration this is fine and true. Read as an algorithm it is unusable: to establish that type_a is a subtype of type_c you must invent an intermediate type_b out of nowhere, and nothing in the rule tells you how. A rule like this can be checked but not executed, so no type checker can be generated from it.

Definition Algorithmic inference rules are the subset of inference rules that are constructive . In P4-SpecTec they are obtained by taking the WebAssembly SpecTec meta-language and adding constraints, so that every rule that type-checks as a rule can also be run as a procedure.

The deck lists four of the constraints and says there are more :

  1. Relations must annotate which of their positions are inputs and which are outputs.
  2. Data must flow through inputs, then premises, then outputs.
  3. All variables must be known, except in binders.
  4. = is disambiguated into ==, := or =:.

The four are one idea seen from four angles: a rule must say which way information travels. Constraint 1 fixes the direction at the level of a relation, constraint 2 fixes it at the level of a rule body, and constraint 3 rules out the guessing that made the subtyping rule unusable, since type_b there is a variable that is neither an input nor bound by any premise. Constraint 4 is the same discipline applied to a single symbol: in a declarative rule = can mean either a test or a definition, and the reading depends on which side the reader already knows, so the meta-language makes you pick between a test and a binding in one direction or the other. The deck lists the constraints without elaborating on them.

The result of writing P4 in that constrained meta-language is a full spec model of the syntax and the type system, excluding P4 annotations, at around 8,000 lines of meta-language . Two things about that figure are worth holding on to. It covers the type system rather than the dynamic semantics, which is consistent with everything the deck goes on to demonstrate. And eight thousand lines is a substantial artifact written by hand, not extracted from anything, which follows directly from P4 having no document to extract from.

What the framework generates

The spec model sits at the top and P4-SpecTec produces backends from it . Five are listed: the official specification, a parser, a type checker, an interpreter and a test suite. The slide is titled "vision and current status" and does not label which of the five are complete, so the honest reading is that the list is the plan and the deck's evidence covers two of the entries.

The type checker backend is the clearest demonstration of what an algorithmic rule buys you . A relation in the spec model, for example relation Stmt_ok: context ⊢ stmt : context with a hint marking positions 0 and 1 as inputs, plus the metalanguage interpreter, together are a P4 type checker. Nothing is compiled or hand-written in between: running the rules is the type checker. It was validated against the p4c reference compiler's test suite, passing 1,063 of 1,067 positive tests, which is 99.6 percent, and 254 of 258 negative tests, which is 98.4 percent.

The second backend generates negative type tests, that is, programs that should be rejected . The motivation is that the official suite leaves many type errors untested, so the backend adds tests for previously untested type errors. Running those against p4c discovered 26 previously unknown bugs in the compiler's frontend. This is the HATRA 2025 result, and it is separate from the FSE numbers later in the deck.

The official specification, the parser and the interpreter appear on the same slide as intended backends. The deck gives no results for them in this lecture, so nothing is claimed here about their state. The specification backend is the interesting one conceptually, since it is the arrow that would let the community keep the prose document and have it be generated rather than maintained: the same relationship ECMA-262 has to ESMeta, but running in the opposite direction.

Careful Two different bug counts appear in this lecture and they should not be added together or confused. The 26 frontend bugs come from the negative test backend in the HATRA 2025 talk . The 15 soundness bugs and 14 crash bugs later on come from the coverage-guided fuzzer in the FSE 2026 paper . They are results of two different pieces of work on the same compiler.

Measuring whether a negative test suite is comprehensive

The second half of the deck is the FSE 2026 paper, "Failing with Purpose: Dangling Coverage-Guided Negative Test Generation from a Mechanized P4 Type System", by Jaehyun Lee, Seokhun Jeong and Sukyoung Ryu . It is also on arXiv as 2608.00639 .

The setup is simple to state . A correct type checker accepts well-typed programs and rejects ill-typed ones, so testing it needs positive tests, which are well-typed programs it should accept, and negative tests, which are ill-typed programs it should reject. Positive testing is comparatively easy, because any program anyone wrote is a candidate. Negative testing is where suites tend to be thin, and thin in a way nobody notices.

The running example is the type rule for shifts . The official specification says the right-hand side of a shift should be of type bit<S>, or, if it is int<S>, then it should be a compile-time known non-negative value.

// well typed: RHS is bit<8>
bit<8> shl(in bit<8> x, in bit<8> y) { return x << y; }

// well typed: RHS is int<8>, but compile-time known and non-negative
bit<8> shl(in bit<8> x) {
    const int<8> y = 42;
    return x << y;
}

// ill typed: RHS is int<8> and not compile-time known
bit<8> shl(in bit<8> x, in int<8> y) { return x << y; }

The third program must be rejected. The finding that motivates the paper is that of the 254 official negative tests, none targets the condition on the shift right-hand side , and there is a compiler bug sitting in exactly that gap. The rule was written down, an implementation got it wrong, and no test in the suite was pointed at it.

So the goal is a comprehensive negative test suite, with at least one test for each ill-typed condition in the P4 type system . The obstacle is that nobody can say what those conditions are . The specification is natural language, ambiguous and incomplete; there is no systematic characterization of the ill-typed conditions; and it is difficult to correlate the negative tests you do have with the conditions they are supposed to exercise. You cannot measure coverage of a set you cannot enumerate.

This is where the mechanized spec model stops being an engineering convenience and becomes the point of the exercise. A machine-readable type system is a set that can be enumerated : it is written in the SpecTec* specification language, it is a formal description of P4 syntax and semantics, it can be parsed and treated as data, and it can be executed, so running the type system spec is running a type checker. The first three capabilities are what make the measure possible; the fourth is what makes the test oracle possible.

The type rule from before, written in the meta-language, looks like this :

syntax binop = PLUS | MINUS | SHL | SHR
syntax type  = IntT | BitT
syntax expr  = VarE text | IntE int | BitE nat | BinE binop expr expr

rule Expr_ok/binary-shift:
  C ⊢ BinE binop expr_l expr_r : type_l
  ----
  -- if binop ∈ { SHL, SHR }
  -- Expr_ok: C ⊢ expr_l : type_l
  -- Expr_ok: C ⊢ expr_r : type_r
  -- if $is_int(type_r) => $is_non_negative(C, expr_r)

Feed a P4 program and this rule to the spec interpreter and you get a verdict : the well-typed shift passes, the ill-typed one fails. Now comes the observation the paper is built on . Inference rules only define well-typed conditions. If a premise is violated, that corresponds to an ill-typed condition. So the premises of the type system look, at first glance, like exactly the enumeration of type errors that was missing.

They are not, quite . Some premises exist only to perform case analysis. The premise binop ∈ { SHL, SHR } in the shift rule has a counterpart, binop ∈ { PLUS, MINUS }, in the rule for addition and subtraction:

rule Expr_ok/binary-shift:
  ...
  -- if binop ∈ { SHL, SHR }        <- a counterpart exists

rule Expr_ok/binary-plus-minus:
  ...
  -- if binop ∈ { PLUS, MINUS }     <- that counterpart

Violating the first of those is not a type error. It just means the other rule applies. This is a direct consequence of the design decision back on slide 20: because the meta-language forbids nested control flow and forces case analysis to be spelled out as separate rules, the case-analysis premises are visible as syntax rather than buried inside an if. That is what makes them findable.

Key idea A dangling premise is a premise with no counterpart . Violating a premise that has a counterpart simply moves you to another rule. Violating a dangling premise leaves nowhere to go, so it is a genuine type error, that is, an ill-typed condition. Filtering the case-analysis premises out of the full set of premises therefore yields a mechanically derived enumeration of the ways a P4 program can fail to type-check.

In the shift rule, if $is_int(type_r) => $is_non_negative(C, expr_r) is dangling. Nothing else in the specification catches a program that violates it, so a program that violates it is ill-typed. And that gives the measure the work needed : run a set of P4 programs against the spec, and dangling coverage is the fraction of the extracted dangling premises that some program in the set actually violates. It quantifies how comprehensive a negative test suite is, against a baseline derived from the specification itself rather than from anybody's judgement.

Why does having a measure change what a fuzzer can do?

Without a measure, generating ill-typed programs is easy but aimless: you can produce a million rejected programs that all break the same handful of rules, and you have no way to tell that this is what happened. Dangling coverage turns test generation into a search with a gradient. A mutation is interesting precisely when it violates a dangling premise that no earlier test violated, which is a condition the tool can evaluate for itself, so the generator can keep the mutations that reach new ground and discard the rest. That is the same move coverage-guided fuzzing made for code paths, applied to conditions in a specification rather than to branches in a program.

The generation loop follows from the measure . It starts from the observation that type errors tend to be one step away from a well-typed program: changing in bit<8> y to in int<8> y in the shift example is a single edit that turns an accepted program into one that violates a dangling premise.

Parse the mechanized spec as data and collect the premises that have no counterpart. These are the conditions the generator is trying to hit, and they are also the coverage domain against which any test suite can be scored.

Take well-typed P4 programs as starting points. The evaluation used 1,166 of them, drawn from the official test suite, which is a nice reuse: the positive tests, which were never intended for this, become the raw material for the negative ones.

Apply a random mutation to a seed. Two choices have to be made here, which well-typed program to mutate and which part of it to mutate, and the deck defers both to the paper rather than describing the strategies .

Run the mutant through the spec interpreter. The question is not merely whether it is rejected but whether it violates a dangling premise that has not been violated before. That check is what makes the search directed rather than a random walk.

Update dangling coverage and go round again, keeping what advanced the measure. The output is a set of negative tests, each of which is tied to the specific ill-typed condition it exercises, which is the correlation that slide 38 said was missing.

What the evaluation found

The deck lists five evaluation questions : whether the approach achieves higher dangling coverage than the official test suite, how effective the mutation strategies are, how good the fuzzer is at finding bugs, how sensitive dangling coverage is to the negative input space, and how it compares with existing P4 fuzzers. Results are shown for the first and third.

On coverage , 939 dangling premises were extracted from the mechanized spec. Ten hours of fuzzing over the 1,166 well-typed seed programs generated 422 negative tests. The official suite, as the baseline, covers 247 of the 939 dangling premises, which is 26.30 percent. The generated tests cover 557 of 939, which is 59.32 percent, an improvement of 33.02 percentage points.

The baseline figure is the one to sit with. Before this work there was no way to know that the official negative suite exercised about a quarter of the conditions the type system defines, because there was no denominator. Producing the denominator is arguably a larger contribution than beating it.

On bug finding , the generated negative tests were run against the official P4 compiler's type checker and found 15 soundness bugs, where the compiler accepts a program it should reject, and 14 crash bugs, where it falls over instead of reporting an error.

The three contributions the deck closes the paper on are worth restating in order, because each depends on the one before it. Define a mechanized formal specification of the P4 type system. Define dangling coverage over that specification, which quantifies the ill-typed conditions. Then generate negative tests guided by that coverage.

The three projects side by side

The closing slide compares all three efforts across the same columns : the problem each one addresses, the solution it offers, and the burden it imposes. The burden column is the honest one, and it is the reason the three projects were not simply the same project done three times.

ProblemSolutionBurden
JavaScriptBugs in the specification and diverse implementationsType checking of the specification, and rich test generationThe ESMeta team's maintenance
WebAssemblyBugs and inconsistencies across artifactsAutomatic generation of the artifactsThe community learning a specification language
P4No specificationUnderstanding, discussing, specifying and testing the semanticsThe community learning a specification language

The slide has one further column, headed "requirement", naming what each effort could build on. For JavaScript that is ECMA-262 and for P4 it is p4c. The WebAssembly cell is an image the deck text does not recover, so it is left out here.

That column is the whole difference in one word. JavaScript's requirement was a document, so ESMeta could extract a model from it and hand the maintenance burden back to a research team, since the committee never had to change how it works. WebAssembly's requirement was a document written by people who wanted it to be formal, so the artifacts could be generated, and the price was that the community had to learn the specification language. P4's requirement is a compiler. There is no document to extract from and no formal standard to generate from, so the solution column reads differently from the other two: not automated production of artifacts, but understanding, discussing, specifying and testing the semantics. The specification has to be created before it can be mechanized, and P4-SpecTec is the vehicle for creating it.

Key idea The lecture's transferable result is not the P4 type checker. It is dangling coverage: given a type system written as algorithmic inference rules, the premises that have no counterpart form a machine-derived enumeration of that language's type errors, and a test suite can be scored against it. Nothing in that argument is specific to P4, or to networking. It needs only a mechanized type system whose case analysis is explicit in the rules, which is precisely what the meta-language design of the previous lecture was already delivering for another reason.
